Part 3

Alignment markers carry the lane identity

Each PCS lane gets a 120-bit alignment marker at a fixed interval. The common bytes are the same on every lane, so the receiver can find AMs without knowing anything else. The unique bytes differ per lane and tell it which PCS lane it found. The pad bytes are ignored on receive.

In 200GBASE-R the AM group repeats every 81,920 257-bit blocks, which is exactly 2,048 codeword pairs: about 2.6 Mbit or 99 µs on each PCS lane. The lab puts a group in every codeword pair so you can watch markers pass. Because AMs are detected in raw lane data before FEC decoding, the receiver tolerates a few bit errors in them.

The group is not laid down marker by marker. Its 960 marker bits are pre-interleaved 10 bits at a time, so that after pre-FEC distribution and symbol distribution each PCS lane's first 12 symbols are exactly its own 120-bit marker. Open the bit lens in the lab to watch it happen.

Part 4

PMA bit-multiplexing

When there are fewer SerDes lanes than PCS lanes, the PMA interleaves PCS lanes bit by bit. Four SerDes lanes carry two PCS lanes each; two carry four each. The PMA picks which PCS lanes share a SerDes lane and holds that choice for as long as the link is up. A given PCS lane never wanders to another SerDes lane during normal operation.

Two consequences matter in the lab. A SerDes lane's skew is shared by every PCS lane it carries, so the lanes slip together. And one bad PAM4 symbol can hit two PCS lanes at once, which is part of why per-PCS-lane error counts tend to rise in groups.

Part 5

Receive: find, line up, reorder, then decode

The RX PMA recovers each SerDes lane and demultiplexes it, but it cannot tell PCS lanes apart, and the bit phase it starts on is arbitrary. So the RX PCS gets 8 streams in an unknown order with unknown relative delay. It then:

  1. Locks on each PMA lane by finding the common AM pattern repeating at the right spacing, and reads the lane number from the unique bytes.
  2. Deskews by buffering early lanes until all AMs line up. 802.3 budgets about 180 ns of skew at the PCS receive input.
  3. Reorders lanes into PCS lane order 0 to 7.
  4. Sets align_status true. Only now can it de-interleave codewords, run RS(544,514) decode, remove AMs, descramble, reverse the transcode and decode 64B/66B.

Deskew and reorder have to come before FEC, because every codeword is spread across all 8 lanes. A single missing lane means no codewords at all, which is why one bad SerDes lane takes the whole link down instead of just its share of the bandwidth.

Part 6

Reading the link from the bottom up

Work in the same order the receiver does. SerDes signal first (CDR lock, signal detect, eye or SNR). Then PCS alignment. Then FEC margin. Then the MAC. Each layer only means something once the one below it is healthy.

Standard concept or vendor telemetry?

IEEE 802.3 defines the ideas: alignment status, per-lane AM lock, the lane mapping, corrected and uncorrected codeword counts, and per-lane FEC symbol error counts, along with management variables for them. Register addresses, counter widths, clear-on-read behaviour, skew readouts, error histograms and BER estimates are up to each device. The lab's diagnostics panel keeps the two groups apart.

Part 7

Beyond 200G

400GBASE-R uses the same Clause 119 PCS with 16 PCS lanes instead of 8, and AM groups every 163,840 257-bit blocks. Everything above carries over at twice the width. For 400G, 16 PCS lanes allow 16, 8, 4, 2 or 1 physical lanes; at 200G, 8 PCS lanes allow 8, 4, 2 or 1.

800G is often pictured as two 400G flows side by side, and the 800GBASE-R PCS does reuse 400G-style FEC groups. Treat that as a mental model. It is not a statement about how any particular device or PHY type is built. Newer PHYs with 200 Gb/s per physical lane also add sublayers not shown here.
