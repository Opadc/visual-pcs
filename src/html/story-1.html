Part 1

Three things called "lane"

Most confusion in PCS debugging comes from one word doing three jobs. A PCS lane is a stream of data with an identity. A PMA lane is a position on the service interface between PCS and PMA. A SerDes lane is a physical path with a driver, a channel and a CDR. They line up one-to-one only in the 8-lane NRZ case.

Where a retimer sits

A retimer or gearbox is a PMA. It recovers each SerDes lane, demultiplexes it into PCS-lane-rate streams, and multiplexes them out again, possibly to a different width. It moves bits without decoding them, so it does not need to know which PCS lane is which. What it must do is keep a fixed mapping while the link is up and stay inside the skew budget.

Part 2

Transmit: one stream becomes eight lanes

On transmit, the PCS works on a single aggregate stream until the very last step. The order is defined by IEEE 802.3 Clause 119, and it matters for debugging because it decides what each later stage can see.

  1. 64B/66B encode each 8 bytes from 200GMII into a 66-bit block. Idles are deleted here to make room for alignment markers.
  2. Transcode four 66-bit blocks into one 256B/257B block. Same data, packed tighter. This is a transcode of the 64B/66B blocks, not a second line code.
  3. Scramble the 257-bit blocks.
  4. Insert alignment markers, one per PCS lane, as a group. They go in after the scrambler, so they stay recognizable in raw lane data.
  5. Pre-FEC distribution: split the stream 10 bits at a time between two Reed-Solomon encoders.
  6. RS(544,514) encode into codewords A and B. AMs are inside the codewords, so FEC protects them.
  7. Symbol distribution: deal 10-bit symbols from both codewords round-robin onto the 8 PCS lanes.

The last step is why the receiver has so much work to do. Each PCS lane carries a slice of every codeword, so nothing can be decoded until all 8 lanes have been found, lined up and put in order.
